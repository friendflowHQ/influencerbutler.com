Most creators put real effort into picking products for their [Benable](https://benable.com/i/CJAQK) lists and then slap a lazy title on top, which is a little like cooking a great meal and serving it in the trash. Your Benable SEO mostly lives in the title. It is the headline that decides whether someone clicks, and it is the text search engines lean on hardest when they decide whether to surface your list at all. Get the title right and a single list can pull in shoppers for months. Get it wrong and your best recommendations sit in the dark.

The good news: titling well is a skill, not a talent, and the rules are simple. Benable lists are discoverable both on Benable itself and through search engines, so a title that matches how real people search is doing two jobs at once. Let's make yours work.

## Title for the search, not for yourself

The most common mistake is titling a list the way *you* think about it instead of the way a *shopper* searches for it. "My faves" makes sense in your head. Nobody types "my faves" into a search bar. They type "best gifts for new dads" or "cheap skincare for oily skin" or "small apartment organization ideas."

So the core move is to write the title as the search someone would actually run. Picture the person, picture what they type, and make that your title. This one shift fixes most weak titles instantly.

Quick before-and-after:

- "Skincare" becomes "Skincare for oily, acne-prone skin"
- "Gift ideas" becomes "Gifts under $50 for the dad who has everything"
- "Home stuff" becomes "Small-space organization picks under $25"
- "Travel" becomes "What I actually pack in my carry-on"

Same products, wildly different odds of being found.

## Use a repeatable title formula

You do not have to invent a great title from scratch every time. A simple formula carries most of the weight:

**[who it's for] + [what they want] + [a qualifier]**

The qualifier is the secret ingredient. It is a price cap, a specific person, a use case, a constraint. "Gifts for mom" is fine. "Gifts under $40 for the mom who's hard to shop for" is specific, searchable, and clickable. Qualifiers narrow the audience, which sounds like a downside but is actually how you match real searches and beat broad, generic lists.

> The mental model: a great list title sounds like a question a friend would ask you, answered in advance. If it reads like a real query, search treats it like one.

## Front-load the words that matter

Search engines and human eyes both weight the start of a title more heavily. Put your most important, most-searched words first, and let the flavor come after.

So "The 12 cozy candles that make my apartment smell amazing" is charming, but "Cozy candles for a fall apartment" leads with the words people search and reads cleaner in a results list. You can keep some personality, just make sure the searchable core is up front, not buried behind a clever intro.

A practical habit: write your title, then ask "if someone only read the first four words, would they know what this list is?" If not, reorder.

## Be specific enough to win, broad enough to matter

There is a balance here. Too broad ("Beauty products") and you are competing with the entire internet and matching nobody in particular. Too narrow ("Vegan lip balm for chapped lips in winter under $8") and almost nobody searches that exact phrase.

Aim for the middle: specific enough to match a real, recurring search, broad enough that a meaningful number of people run it. Good middles look like:

- "Drugstore skincare that actually works"
- "Gifts for coffee lovers under $30"
- "Beginner running gear that's worth it"
- "Cozy reading nook essentials"

## Match the title to the list (and keep your promise)

A title is a promise. If you title a list "Gifts under $50" and half the items cost $90, people bounce, and bounces hurt both your conversions and how the list gets surfaced over time. Make the title true. If your list is eight cozy candles, the title should be about cozy candles, not "my whole fall vibe."

This also means one tight theme per list. A list trying to be "skincare and makeup and hair and a few home things" cannot have a title that matches any single search well. Split it. Three focused lists with three sharp, searchable titles will out-earn one grab-bag list with a vague title every time.

## Refresh titles that underperform

Titles are not set in stone. If a list has been live for a while and gets little traffic, the title is the first thing to test. Try rewriting it around a different search angle: swap the qualifier, lead with a different keyword, make it more specific. A list that was invisible under one title can start getting found under a better one, with the exact same products inside.
