Good Amazon product research is the difference between content that has to fight for every sale and content that practically sells itself. New creators spend most of their energy on the wrong end of this: better lighting, better editing, a snappier hook. All of that helps a little. But the single biggest lever on your commissions is upstream, in the products you choose to feature in the first place. Pick items with built-in demand and your content does less convincing. Pick poorly and no amount of polish saves it.

So let's talk about how to actually find products that sell themselves. Not in the "secret arbitrage tool" sense, but in the practical, do-this-today sense. The goal is to feature things that already have shoppers, healthy commissions, and momentum, so you are riding existing demand instead of trying to manufacture it.

## Start with demand you can see

The best products to feature are the ones people are already buying. That sounds obvious, but most creators feature what *they* think is cool rather than what shoppers are actually purchasing. Your job is to find the overlap, and Amazon hands you the signals for free if you know where to look.

- **Best Seller Rank.** A strong rank in a category means consistent sales volume. You want products on pages that shoppers are already landing on.
- **Review count and velocity.** Lots of reviews means lots of buyers. A product steadily gaining new reviews is one with current momentum, not yesterday's hit.
- **Rating quality.** A solid star rating means fewer disappointed buyers, which means fewer returns clawing back your commission later.
- **Price point.** Mid-range "treat yourself" prices often convert better than either cheap impulse junk or expensive items people deliberate over for weeks.

None of this requires paid software. It is sitting on the listing in plain sight. The skill is reading those signals together instead of falling for a pretty product with no buyers behind it.

## Match the product to buyer intent

Demand is only half the picture. The other half is whether the people seeing your content are in a buying mood for *this* thing. A product can be a runaway best seller and still flop in your content if your audience has no intent toward it.

The cleanest test is your own audience behavior. What have people bought from you before? What do they ask about? What problems do they keep mentioning? Buyer intent is highest when a product solves a specific, felt problem for the exact people watching you. Our guide on [choosing products that convert](/blog/choose-products-that-convert) digs into the intent side of this in more detail, but the short version is: feature solutions to problems your audience already has, not products you simply find neat.

> A best-selling product your audience has zero intent toward is still zero dollars. Demand and intent have to meet. Research is just finding where they overlap.

## Favor commission-friendly categories

Two products can sell at the same rate and pay you wildly differently, because Amazon's commission rates swing hard by category. This is the quiet factor that decides whether your effort compounds or stalls.

You do not need to memorize the whole rate card, but you should know roughly where your niche sits and lean toward the better-paying corners of it when you have a choice. A 30-second onsite video takes the same effort whether the payout is tiny or healthy, so all else equal, point your effort at the products and categories that pay. Over a few hundred pieces of content, that single bias is the difference between a hobby and an income.

## Watch the momentum, not just the snapshot

A best seller today might be a fading product tomorrow. The creators who consistently pick winners pay attention to *direction*, not just the current numbers.

1. **Rising review velocity** signals a product on its way up. Getting in early on something gaining steam means your evergreen content rides the wave.
2. **Steady, durable demand** beats a one-week spike. A product people buy every month earns you forever. A flash-in-the-pan dies and takes your content's relevance with it.
3. **Seasonal timing.** Some products predictably surge at certain times of year. Featuring them a few weeks ahead of the wave, not during it, gets your content ranking before the rush.

This is where seasonal keyword thinking pays off. Knowing what is about to be in demand, and lining up content before the surge, is a genuine edge most creators never use because the research feels like too much work.

## A repeatable research routine

To keep this from being abstract, here is a loop you can actually run each week.

1. **Start from your sales.** List the products your audience has actually bought from you. That is your highest-confidence pool.
2. **Find adjacent winners.** For each, look at similar products with strong ranks, good ratings, and rising reviews.
3. **Filter by commission.** Drop anything in a category that pays poorly unless the demand is overwhelming.
4. **Layer in timing.** Flag anything about to hit a seasonal surge and prioritize it now, ahead of the wave.
5. **Pick your next batch and make content.** Then check what earned, and feed that back into next week's list.

Run that loop consistently and your hit rate climbs every month, because you are compounding real signals instead of restarting from scratch.
