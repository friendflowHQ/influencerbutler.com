In the last post in this series, I gave you the rule I hold myself to: no more than 5 accounts on a single residential IP. A lot of people wrote back with some version of the same question. "How do I actually know how many accounts are really on my IP?" That is a fair question, and the honest answer is uncomfortable: you often cannot tell just by looking at your invoice. So this post goes one layer deeper than the last one, into the part of this business almost nobody talks about out loud.

## Paying for the right thing is not the same as getting it

Here is what eleven years and six figures on proxy infrastructure taught me, the hard way, more than once: the type of proxy you buy and what you actually receive are two different questions, and a lot of people only ever check the first one.

Some providers sell the exact same datacenter IP to more than one customer at the same time. Every account posting through that address gets tied together in the platform's eyes, so one other customer's bad behavior on "your" IP becomes your problem too, even though you never touched their account.

"Private" and "dedicated" are labels on a price list, not a guarantee. I have seen datacenter proxies marketed as private or dedicated that were still shared on the back end whenever the provider was short on inventory. The label tells you what you were sold. It does not tell you what you are actually running on.

> A price list calls it private. That does not make it private. Only the provider's own infrastructure decides that, and most providers will not volunteer the answer unless you ask directly.

And residential proxies are not automatically exempt from this either. You think you bought one address for your 5 accounts. If another customer is quietly sharing that same address, the real number of accounts funneling through it is higher than you think, and every account on it gets flagged faster than the math in your head says it should.

Think of it like paying extra for your own locked mailbox at the shipping place, the kind with a key only you are supposed to have. Then one day you find out the front desk cut a spare key for somebody else's mail too, because it was easier than finding you a different box. You paid for private. What you got was shared, and you only find out once something in that mailbox gets flagged.

## The best fix most people cannot get, and the one everyone can

In my experience, the most reliable residential providers are not the ones with the biggest ad budget or the flashiest storefront. They are the ones other operators quietly refer each other to, word of mouth, after years of actually using them. I cannot hand that referral to everyone reading this, and honestly, even if I could, that is not really the point of this post.

The real value here is not a name. It is knowing how to check any provider yourself, the big public ones included, so you are never just trusting a sales page.

## How to vet a proxy provider yourself

This works whether you are looking at a big-name storefront or a small operator a friend mentioned. Go through it before you hand anyone money, not after something goes wrong.

- Ask in writing: is this IP exclusive to me, and is it ever resold or shared on the back end?
- Ask how big the pool is and how many customers draw from it.
- Check the IP yourself before you use it: run it through a free IP reputation checker and see if it comes back flagged as datacenter, VPN, or proxy.
- Check the ASN and ISP name on that same lookup. Does it belong to an actual home internet provider, or to a hosting company wearing a residential-sounding name?
- Ask whether the IP is sticky or rotating. For an account you keep signed in, you want the same address every time, not a new one on every request.
- Ask how the residential IPs are sourced, and whether the people whose home connections are being used actually agreed to it. A provider that can answer this cleanly, an ethically sourced pool, tends to also be the more stable one.
- Ask what the replacement policy is if an IP you are assigned ever gets flagged or burned.
- Watch for red flags: prices that seem too cheap to be real, "unlimited" anything, vague or dodged answers, and any reluctance to put exclusivity in writing.
- Start with one low-value account on any new provider before you move anything that actually matters onto it.

None of this means every public proxy storefront is running a scam. Most are not. It means vetting matters regardless of where you buy, because the sales page cannot tell you what is happening on the provider's own back end. Only asking, and checking, can.

## The takeaway

A proxy being the right type, residential instead of datacenter, is the first filter, not the last one. Even the right type, bought from a real provider, at a fair price, can still be shared in ways the label on the price list never mentions. Ask the questions above in writing before you buy, check the IP yourself once you have it, and start small with anyone new.
