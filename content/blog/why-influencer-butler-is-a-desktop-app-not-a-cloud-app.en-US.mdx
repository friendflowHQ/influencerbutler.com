I've been building automation for creators and Amazon sellers since 2011. In that time I've personally spent more than $100,000 on proxy infrastructure, running "the computer does it for you" setups for real clients, at real scale. So when I see a company call running a browser on a server "groundbreaking," I want to save you some money and some heartbreak, because I already paid for this lesson so you don't have to.

Yesterday I wrote about [what "works while your computer is off" actually means](/blog/cloud-automation-data-center-proxy-ban-risk). This is the longer version: what cloud automation really is, the part about proxies that nobody likes to say out loud, and why I built [Influencer Butler](/signup) to run on your own computer instead of ours.

## First, the plain-English version of what we're actually talking about

A "server" or "cloud computer" is just a computer somewhere else that's always turned on. Instead of your laptop running the automation, someone else's computer does, sitting in a data center, awake 24/7.

That part genuinely isn't new. Amazon Web Services has been renting out exactly this since 2006. Cloud computing is two decades old. Calling it groundbreaking in 2026 is like calling email groundbreaking. It isn't the innovation. It's just where the automation happens to be standing.

## Here's the part that actually matters: the proxy

When that remote computer connects to Amazon (or Instagram, or anywhere else) to act like it's you, it needs an IP address, basically a return address that says "this traffic came from here." There are a few kinds, and the difference between them is the entire ballgame.

- **Datacenter IP.** Think of it like mail coming from a known shipping warehouse instead of a person's house. Amazon, Instagram, and every major platform keep lists of these addresses, and traffic from them gets extra scrutiny before anything else even happens. It's the cheapest option, and it's cheap for a reason: everyone who's ever actually run automation at scale knows it's the first thing that gets flagged.
- **Residential IP.** This is a real address, tied to a real home internet connection, the kind an actual person uses to check email and watch Netflix. It looks like a normal customer because, somewhere, it is one. This is what anyone who's been doing this seriously for years actually uses, not because it's trendy, but because it's the only option that doesn't get you flagged by default.
- **Mobile IP.** Similar idea, but riding on a phone's cell connection instead of home wifi. Even more trusted on some platforms, and priced accordingly.

> Every cloud automation tool is using one of these. The only question is which one, and whether they'll tell you.

## The part nobody in this space likes to admit out loud

Even residential isn't a blank check.

In all my years doing this, the rule I've held myself to, and the rule any experienced operator holds to, is **no more than 5 accounts sharing a single residential IP.** Not because it's a nice guideline. Because I watched what happens when you push past it: platforms notice multiple separate identities funneling through the same address, and the whole batch gets treated as suspicious together.

One bad account can take down every account sharing its IP with it. I've seen it happen. It's expensive, and it's not reversible.

Now do the math on a cloud automation product. If a company has a few hundred customers and is running all of them from a shared pool of addresses, either they are paying for a lot of dedicated residential IPs (expensive, and you'd expect them to say so, because it's a selling point), or a lot of customers are sharing each address, or they're on datacenter IPs. There isn't a fourth option.

## What silence usually means

So when someone tells you their "cloud automation" is secure, safe, and groundbreaking, and doesn't tell you which of the above it's actually using, here's what that silence usually means in practice: either they don't know (which, for a company literally building this product, would be its own problem), or they do know and the honest answer isn't one they want to say out loud.

A real answer costs nothing to give. "We use dedicated residential IPs, capped at this many accounts per IP" is one sentence. Nobody avoids one sentence by accident.

## What to actually ask, before you hand any tool your Amazon login

1. **Is this running from my own connection, or theirs?**
2. **If theirs, is it residential or datacenter?** (If they don't know this distinction, that's an answer too.)
3. **If residential, how many other customers share that same IP?**
4. **What happens to my login if their servers are ever compromised?**

If a company can answer all four clearly, that's worth something. If they can't, or they respond with a paragraph about innovation and fear instead of an actual answer, you already have your answer.

## Why Influencer Butler is a desktop app, on purpose

People ask us this fairly often, usually phrased as "why do I have to leave my computer on?" It's a fair question, because leaving a computer on is a real inconvenience, and a cloud tool would be easier to sell. Here is the honest reasoning.

**Your account posts from your own connection.** Influencer Butler is a real app you install on your own computer. When it accepts a Creator Connections campaign, sends a brand message, or posts a deal to your Facebook group, that traffic leaves from your home internet connection, the same address you use to shop on Amazon yourself. To the platform it looks like you, because it is you. There's no shared pool, no warehouse address, and nobody else's account riding on your IP.

**Your login never leaves your machine.** Your Amazon and social logins live in your own browser session on your own computer. We don't store them on a server, because there is no server of ours running your account. That's the whole answer to question four. If something of ours were ever compromised, there'd be no Amazon login to take.

**You can see it for yourself, right in the app.** Influencer Butler checks the address it's actually posting from and shows it to you in Settings, in a row called **Current connection**, with a **Re-check** button next to it. If it ever looks like a data center or VPN address (which can happen if you run the app on a rented cloud PC, or forget a VPN is on), the app warns you right there and in the run log before it posts. That's the visibility I wish every tool offered, and it's built in because I didn't want "trust us" to be the answer.

**If you want 24/7, you still own the setup.** Some people genuinely need the butlers running around the clock on a machine that isn't their laptop. We support that too, but the honest way: you rent [your own cloud PC](/blog/run-influencer-butler-free-cloud-pc), you add your own dedicated residential proxy, and you click **Test Connection** to see the real home-style address your account will post from. One account, one address, nobody sharing it. Yes, that's an extra step. The extra step is the point. If you'd rather keep it simple, [keep the app running on your own laptop while you travel](/blog/keep-your-butler-running-while-traveling) and skip the cloud entirely.

So, to answer our own four questions: it runs from your connection; if you choose a cloud PC, you pick a residential address; zero other customers share it; and there's no login on our servers to lose.

## The trade-off, stated plainly

A desktop app means your computer (or a computer you control) has to be on for the butlers to work. That's a real cost, and I'm not going to pretend it isn't. What you get in exchange is that the single most important variable in whether automation gets your account flagged, the address it connects from, is one you control and can see at all times.

After $100,000 and fifteen years of learning this the hard way, that's not marketing. It's just the only way I'd trust it myself.
