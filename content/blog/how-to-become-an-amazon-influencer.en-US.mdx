## What the Amazon Influencer Program actually is

The Amazon Influencer Program is an upgraded version of Amazon's classic affiliate program (Associates). You get two things Associates members do not:

- **A storefront**: your own page on Amazon (amazon.com/shop/yourhandle) full of products you recommend, earning you a commission on purchases made through it.
- **Onsite placement**: after a second approval step, your product review videos appear directly on Amazon product pages, in front of shoppers who are already deciding whether to buy.

Onsite placement is the part that changes everything for beginners, because it means **you do not need an audience**. Amazon puts your videos in front of its own shoppers. You film honest reviews of products you own; Amazon supplies the traffic; you earn a slice of the sales your videos help close.

It costs nothing to join. Anyone charging you for "access" to the program is selling you a free thing.

## Step 1: Meet the requirements and apply

You apply with one social media account: YouTube, Instagram, Facebook, or TikTok. Amazon does not publish exact follower thresholds, and its review is partly automated, but the consistent pattern across thousands of applicants:

- **Engagement beats follower count.** An active account with 1,500 real followers regularly beats a dormant one with 10,000.
- **YouTube and Facebook approvals tend to be the most forgiving.**
- A public account with a real profile, real posts, and some product-adjacent content (things you use, hauls, favorites) reads as a creator; an empty one does not.

Apply at Amazon's influencer page with the Amazon account you actually shop with. If you get rejected: apply again with a different platform account, or spend 30 days posting consistently and reapply. Rejection is common and recoverable, and buying followers makes it worse, not better.

## Step 2: Get approved for onsite video placement

Joining the program gets you the storefront. The money for beginners is behind a second gate: Amazon reviews your first product videos before your content is allowed on product pages.

Your videos pass when they are genuinely useful to a shopper:

1. **Show the real product in use.** Not just the box, not just your face talking.
2. **Film horizontal**, in a quiet room, with decent light. A phone is plenty.
3. **Never mention price, discounts, or promotions** in the video. Prices change; Amazon rejects videos that reference them.
4. **No watermarks from other platforms.** A TikTok logo is an instant fail.
5. **Keep it 60 to 120 seconds**: what it is, how it works, your honest verdict.

Most rejections come from breaking one of those five rules. Fix and resubmit; nothing is permanent.

## Step 3: Film simple videos that convert

You are not becoming a YouTuber. A shopper on a product page wants one thing: a real person showing them the product before they spend money. The formula that works, over and over:

- **Hook (5 to 10 seconds)**: what the product is and the question you will answer.
- **Demo (60 to 90 seconds)**: the product doing its job, including the annoying parts.
- **Verdict (10 to 20 seconds)**: who should buy it, who should skip it.

You do not need to show your face; hands, product, and voice are enough. The real cheat code is **batch filming**: collect 10 to 20 products from around the house, film them all in one afternoon, and upload across the week. Creators who succeed are not more talented; they are more consistent. Every video is an asset that can earn for years, and libraries of a few hundred videos are what sit behind those "I made $3-4k this month" screenshots.

## Step 4: Upload, tag, and title carefully

When you upload a video, you tag the product it shows. Tagging is how Amazon decides which product pages your video appears on and which sales you get credit for, so tag the exact product (and every product that appears, in a comparison). Titles show up in the carousel next to other creators' videos; specific beats generic every time. "Honest review after 6 months of daily use" gets the click that "My review!" does not.

## Step 5: Build a storefront worth browsing

Your storefront earns from traffic you send it, so treat it as your home base rather than passive income: same handle as your socials, a one-line bio that says who you help, and a few specific idea lists ("Small kitchen essentials," not "Stuff I like") of 8 to 15 products you can genuinely vouch for. Put the link in every bio you have. Seasonal lists, built two or three weeks ahead of the season, earn in bursts.

## Step 6: Read your reports without obsessing

Your dashboard reports lag a day or two, and payments arrive monthly, about 60 days behind. The only three numbers a beginner needs: views (is placement working?), ordered items (which videos convert?), and the earnings trend across weeks. Check once or twice a week on a schedule, note what is working, film more of that, and close the tab. Checking stats releases the same dopamine as making progress while producing none of it.

## Your first 30 days, compressed

- **Week 1**: apply; list 20+ products you own; film your first three videos.
- **Week 2**: submit for onsite approval; film five more while you wait; set up the storefront and your first idea list.
- **Week 3**: put a recurring batch-filming block in your calendar; upload with careful tags; add the storefront link to your bios.
- **Week 4**: reach 20 uploaded videos; publish a second idea list; review your first stats and double down on what got views.

Do that and you are ahead of the vast majority of people who join the program and stall. The next eleven months are the same loop with bigger numbers.
