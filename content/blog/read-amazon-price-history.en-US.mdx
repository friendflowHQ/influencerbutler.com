Here is an uncomfortable truth about deal posting: a lot of the "deals" floating around are not deals at all. A product gets quietly bumped from $40 to $60, sits there for a week, then drops "40% off" back to $40. The big red discount badge is real. The savings are fiction. If you post that, you have handed your audience a fake markdown, and the one thing a deal account cannot survive is being wrong about the price. Reading Amazon price history is how you avoid that trap. It tells you the true baseline so the discount you post is a discount your audience can trust.

This guide covers how price history works, how to read it fast, and how to build verification into your posting flow so a fake deal never slips through.

## What price history actually tells you

Every product on Amazon has a story behind its current price, and that story is the price history: the line of where the price has actually been over weeks and months. A current price means nothing on its own. The same $30 is a fantastic deal on an item that normally sells for $60 and a terrible one on an item that has bounced between $22 and $30 all year.

Price history answers the only question that matters for a deal:

- **What is the true normal price?** Not the inflated "list price," the actual price shoppers have been paying.
- **Is the current price genuinely low, or just average dressed up?**
- **How low does this item realistically go?** If it routinely dips to $18, then $25 is not the deal to wait for or to post.

Read the history and the marketing badge stops mattering. You see the real number.

## How to read a price history chart in ten seconds

You do not need to study a chart like an analyst. You are looking for three things, fast.

1. **The recent baseline.** Where has the price mostly lived over the last month or two? That flat band is the real "normal," the number your discount should be measured against.
2. **The all-time low.** The lowest the price has ever dipped. If today's price is at or near it, you have a genuinely strong deal worth posting loudly.
3. **A suspicious recent spike.** Watch for a price that jumped up shortly before the "sale." That is the classic fake-markdown pattern: inflate, wait, then "discount" back to normal.

If the current price sits well below a stable baseline and near the all-time low, post it with confidence. If it is merely back to its usual number after a recent bump, skip it. Your audience will never know how many fake deals you spared them, which is exactly the point.

> The discount badge is a marketing claim. The price history is the receipt. Always trust the receipt.

## The fake markdown patterns to watch for

Once you start reading history, you spot the same tricks over and over. A few worth knowing by name:

- **The pre-sale bump.** Price quietly rises a week before a "sale," then drops back to where it always was. The most common one.
- **The phantom list price.** A "was $99, now $49" where nothing ever actually sold at $99. The crossed-out number is invented.
- **The yo-yo.** A product that constantly flips between two prices so the higher one always looks like a deal when it returns to the lower.
- **The barely-there discount.** A real but trivial drop dressed up with a big percentage off the inflated list price.

None of these survive a glance at the history. That glance is the entire skill.

## Why this matters more than any single great deal

It is tempting to think one fake deal is no big deal. It is. Deal accounts run entirely on trust. People follow you because when you say something is a steal, it is. The first time a follower clips your coupon, checks out, and realizes the price is the same as last week, that trust takes a hit. A few of those and they stop clicking your links, which is the same as them leaving.

Posting only verified deals is slower. It means you reject a lot of finds that looked exciting until you checked the history. But the rejected fakes are exactly what protects the account. The discipline of "verify before you post, always" is what separates a deals creator people rely on from one they quietly mute. ## Building verification into your workflow

Reading price history is simple. Doing it on every single product, every day, dozens of times, is where it gets heavy. The verification step is non-negotiable, but it is also the most tedious part of the job, and tedium is what makes deal posters quit.

The fix is to make verification fast and habitual rather than a chore you dread. Check the history the moment a find catches your eye, before you get attached to it. Reject ruthlessly.
