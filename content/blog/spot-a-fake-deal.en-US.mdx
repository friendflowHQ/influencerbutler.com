You found it. A pair of headphones marked "62% off," the original price slashed through in red, the deal price sitting there looking irresistible. You write the post, drop the link, hit publish. And then a follower replies: "It was this exact price last week." Now you look like you either didn't check or didn't care. That is how you spot a fake deal too late, and it is exactly the moment that quietly chips away at the trust you spent months building.

Here is the uncomfortable truth about deal posting: the discount you see is not always the discount that exists. Sellers inflate "list prices" all the time, prices bounce around daily, and a number that looks like a steal is sometimes just Tuesday's regular price wearing a costume. Learning to tell the difference is the single most important skill a deal poster has, because one fake deal undoes a dozen real ones.

## Why fake deals are everywhere

Most fake deals are not malicious. They are just the natural result of how Amazon pricing works. A seller sets a "List Price" that may never have been the real selling price. The actual price floats up and down based on competition, stock, and time of day. So when you see "$50, was $130," that $130 might be a number the product hasn't sold at in a year, or ever.

There are a few flavors of fake deal worth knowing:

- **The inflated list price.** The strikethrough number is fiction. The "discount" is measured against a price nobody actually paid.
- **The fake low.** The price drops a few dollars below its normal range and gets dressed up as a major markdown.
- **The yo-yo.** The price was this low last week, went up, and came back down. Technically a "deal," but your audience saw it five days ago.
- **The disappearing coupon.** A clipped coupon makes the price look great in your screenshot, but it expires or runs out before your followers click.

None of these are illegal. They just make for a bad post. Your job is to catch them before your audience does.

## The red flags that should slow you down

Most fakes give themselves away if you know where to look. Before you write a single word of a deal post, run through these.

### A discount that seems too clean

A flat "70% off" on a brand-name product that never goes on sale is suspicious. Real markdowns are usually messier: 43% off, 28% off, odd numbers that come from a real price meeting a real discount. When the math is suspiciously round and suspiciously huge, the list price is probably doing the heavy lifting, not the actual savings.

### A "was" price you can't verify anywhere

If the strikethrough price doesn't match what the product sold for recently, treat it as decoration. The question is never "what does Amazon say it was?" The question is "what did this actually cost last month?" Those are different numbers more often than you'd think.

### A price that's been here before

A deal is only a deal relative to its own history. If the item has bounced to this price three times this quarter, it is not a deal, it is just the price. Your audience is smart, and the regulars who follow deal accounts have long memories.

> A real deal answers one question cleanly: is this meaningfully cheaper than it has actually been recently? If you can't answer yes with confidence, it's not ready to post.

## How to verify a deal in under a minute

You don't need to become a forensic accountant. A quick verification habit takes less time than writing the caption.

1. **Check the real price history.** Use a price-tracking tool or browser extension that shows the product's true price over time, not just Amazon's list price. Look for where the current price sits against the genuine recent low. If you want a deeper walkthrough of this, our guide on [reading Amazon price history](/blog/catch-amazon-price-drops) covers what the chart is actually telling you.
2. **Ignore the strikethrough.** Mentally delete the "was" price and ask whether the current price is good on its own. If the item is great value at its real price, say that instead of leaning on a fake markdown.
3. **Confirm the coupon is live and stocked.** If the deal depends on a clipped coupon, make sure it is still available and not about to vanish. A coupon that runs out mid-post makes everyone who clicked feel cheated.
4. **Sanity-check the seller and reviews.** A "deal" on a no-name listing with three reviews is a different risk than a price drop on an established product. Steep discounts on sketchy listings are sometimes bait.

Run those four and you'll catch the overwhelming majority of fakes. The whole point of [posting deals as an influencer](/blog/deal-posting-influencer-guide) is that people trust your eye. Verification is how you protect that eye.

## When the deal is real but borderline

Not every gray-area deal is a fake. Sometimes the discount is modest but the product is genuinely good, or the price is the lowest it's been in six months even if it's not a record. Those are postable, as long as you're honest about them. "Lowest I've seen since spring" is a true, useful framing. "70% off" when it isn't is not.

The rule of thumb: describe the deal as it actually is. A smaller, honest claim outperforms a bigger, fake one every time, because the honest one keeps people clicking your links next week too.
